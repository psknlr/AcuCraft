id: red-flags-and-referral
title: Red flags, risk and the decision not to needle
when: Before you decide to treat, and at once when an answer, a finding or a report worries you.
---
Acupuncture is elective. The first question of every visit is whether this patient should be needled today at all.

**Screen.** Ask the questions whose answers would send the patient elsewhere, for the complaint in front of you: fever and feeling unwell; a joint that is hot, red and swollen; a recent injury; weakness, numbness, loss of bladder or bowel control; a sudden, severe or changing headache; chest pain or breathlessness; low mood and thoughts of self-harm; for a patient under treatment for cancer, fever and the latest blood counts. Read the records they brought: a blood count or an INR is asked for by reading it, not by asking how they feel.

**Recognise on evidence.** `lookup vocabulary` lists the red flags the clinic names. Record the ones you recognise in `assess` (`redFlags`). You can only recognise what you have evidence of: the opening statement, an answer, a finding, a report. A flag asserted without having asked is a guess; a flag you asked about and did not find is not there.

**Act on it.**
- An emergency (a possible infection in a joint, nerve compression with bladder or bowel signs, a neurological emergency, a patient at risk of harming themselves): no needles. Tell the patient what you are worried about and what happens now (`say`), `refer` with the urgency it needs — `lookup clinic` for the targets — document, `end_visit`.
- A reason to wait (blood counts too low to needle safely, an unclear diagnosis another clinician should settle first): `decide` defer, tell the patient, liaise with the team that treats them (`refer`, the urgency that fits), document.
- A risk that changes how you treat, not whether: `decide` treat-modified and change the plan — fewer needles, shallower, pressure after removal for someone who bleeds easily; other points for a limb with lymphoedema or broken skin; a position the patient can hold.

**Points and regions to avoid.** A point's cautions (`lookup point`) name the conditions it should not be needled in, and the rules in force (`lookup rules`) can keep needles out of whole regions for a patient with a risk factor. A rule about a region is about where the needle goes: calling the spot an Ashi point does not move it.

**The patient's own limits.** Hungry, exhausted, very anxious or afraid of needles: more likely to faint. Lying is safer than sitting. A patient who declines a position has declined it; find another or do not treat there.

Referring or deferring when the findings call for it is the correct outcome of the visit. Needling a patient who should have been referred is the serious error; so is sending away, unexamined, a patient with no reason not to be treated.
